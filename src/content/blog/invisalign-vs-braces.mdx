Invisalign and braces both straighten teeth, and both count as orthodontic treatment, the branch of dentistry that moves teeth and lines up the bite. Your real choice is between clear aligners you can take out and fixed braces that stay on until the job is done.

Which one fits depends on your teeth, your habits, and your goals. First, though, a simpler question.

## First question: do you need treatment at all?

Straightening isn't always necessary, and we'll tell you honestly whether you need it.

Crooked teeth can cause real health problems. Crowded teeth are harder to brush and floss than straight ones, so plaque builds up in the tight spots and raises the risk of cavities and gum disease. A bad bite, meaning the way your top and bottom teeth meet, can wear down enamel, chip edges, or strain the jaw joint, and any of those is a good reason to treat.

Other concerns are cosmetic: a small gap, one tooth that sits a little forward, a slight twist you only notice in photos. But cosmetic concerns open up smaller options than full orthodontic treatment.

Say you have one small gap between your front teeth and an otherwise healthy bite. Bonding might close it in a single visit: we shape tooth-colored resin onto the sides of the teeth, with little or no drilling. Or maybe your teeth have shifted a tiny bit since high school and it doesn't bother you, in which case we'd keep an eye on it at your regular cleanings.

Nobody should start orthodontics because a friend did, or because an ad made it look easy. Begin with an exam and a frank talk about what's going on. You'll find the longer version of how we think about this in our post on [what conservative dentistry means](/blog/what-is-conservative-dentistry).

## How each one works

Both apply gentle, steady pressure that moves teeth a little at a time, using different tools.

### Clear aligners

Aligners are thin, clear plastic trays made to fit your teeth. Planning starts with a digital scan of your mouth, and we map each small movement ahead of time. We shape each tray slightly differently from the one before. On a set schedule, you switch to the next tray, and your teeth follow along.

Certain movements need something for the trays to grip, so we bond small tooth-colored bumps, called attachments, onto some of your teeth. They're made of a resin like the one used in bonding, and we remove them at the end. Not every patient needs them, and most people stop noticing them after a few days.

More detail is on our [Invisalign page](/services/orthodontic-services/invisalign).

### Braces

Braces use small brackets glued to the front of each tooth, joined by a thin wire. The wire does the pushing and pulling. During each visit, the orthodontist adjusts or swaps it to keep teeth moving in the right direction. Rubber bands or other small parts can help correct how the jaws line up.

Metal and tooth-colored brackets are both available. You can't take them out, and neither can your kid.

## Who's a good fit for each

Aligners suit mild to moderate problems best. Fixed braces handle a wider range, including the hard cases.

Mild to moderate crowding and spacing respond well to aligners, as do some mild bite issues. This covers adults who had braces as kids and saw their teeth drift, plus teens with straightforward alignment concerns.

Harder cases, like a large overbite or underbite or jaws that don't line up well, often call for braces. Teeth that need to turn a long way also tend to do better in braces, especially rounder teeth that aligners have a harder time gripping. Big vertical moves, like pulling a tooth down into place, fall in the same group.

Habits matter as much as teeth. Trays only work while they're in your mouth. Picture a teen who takes them out at lunch and leaves them in a backpack all afternoon: that teen won't get the result on the plan. Kids who forget often do better in braces, because braces can't come out. No knock on teenagers. Adults forget too.

Some cases fall in between, and then we'll talk through both paths with you.

## The discipline aligners require

People underestimate this part. Aligners need to be in your mouth about 20 to 22 hours a day, which leaves two to four hours for eating, drinking anything other than water, and brushing.

Three meals and a couple of coffees use up that time fast.

Forget often, and the teeth fall behind the plan, the next tray won't fit right, and treatment runs longer. Missing an hour now and then won't ruin anything. Still, steady wear is the single biggest factor in whether aligners work.

Teen sets from [Invisalign for Teens](/services/orthodontic-services/invisalign-for-teens) often include small wear indicators that fade with use. They give you and us a simple way to see whether the trays are staying in.

## Daily life with each option

Here's the plain trade: aligners ask more of your habits, and braces ask more of your menu and your brushing routine.

| | Clear aligners | Braces |
|---|---|---|
| Removable? | Out for meals and brushing | No |
| Eating | Take trays out, eat anything, brush before putting them back | Avoid hard, sticky, and chewy foods |
| Cleaning | Brush and floss as usual | Takes longer; floss threaders or water flossers help |
| Sports | Take out or wear a mouthguard, as advised | Wear an orthodontic mouthguard |
| Look | Hard to see | Visible, though tooth-colored brackets are an option |

### Eating and drinking

Aligner wearers take the trays out, eat what they like, then brush and put them back. Sipping soda or juice with trays in traps the sugar against your teeth for hours. Please don't.

Hard foods like nuts, popcorn, and ice can break a bracket. Caramel and other sticky foods get stuck in the wires.

### Speech, comfort, and sports

Expect a slight lisp for the first few days with aligners. It usually fades as your tongue gets used to them. Each new tray feels tight for a day or two, which is a sign it's doing its job.

Early on, braces often rub the inside of the cheeks and lips, and a bit of orthodontic wax over a bracket helps. Soreness for a few days after each adjustment is normal.

Anyone with braces should wear a mouthguard for sports, since a hit to the mouth can cut the lips on the brackets. With aligners, you can often take the trays out for a game or wear a guard.

## Protecting your enamel and gums during treatment

Straight teeth with new cavities aren't a good trade. Cleaning habits matter more during orthodontics than at almost any other time.

Brackets create small ledges where plaque collects. Left there, it can leave chalky white spots on the enamel, an early sign of mineral loss that can stay visible after the braces come off. Careful brushing around every bracket and cleaning under the wire keep them from forming.

Plastic trays bring their own risk, because they hold whatever is on your teeth right against them. Brush before you put them back in, rinse the trays, and save sugary drinks for times when the trays are out. Gums need attention too. Puffy, bleeding gums slow things down and make treatment less comfortable.

Keep up your regular cleanings and exams throughout. We'd rather catch a problem spot in month three than find it on the day you finish.

## Visits and timelines

Both options take months, and the length depends on your teeth.

Aligner patients get trays in sets and come in for periodic check-ins so we can confirm the teeth are tracking. Sometimes the plan needs a small correction partway through, so we rescan and make a few extra trays.

Regular visits to the orthodontist for wire adjustments come with braces. Those visits are part of how the teeth move, and skipping them slows everything down.

Simple cases finish sooner than complex ones, whichever option you choose. Until we've looked at your teeth, we won't quote a timeline, because any estimate before then would be a guess.

## Pulling teeth: a last resort

Some orthodontic plans call for removing healthy teeth to make room. Severe crowding can make that the right call, since there may be no other way to fit all the teeth in a good position.

Our preference is to avoid it when possible. Gently widening the arch can make room. Removing a thin sliver of enamel between some teeth, a step called interproximal reduction, is another way. Interproximal reduction does take away a small amount of enamel, so we only suggest it when the trade makes sense, and we explain it first. If someone recommends removing healthy teeth for you or your child, ask why and what the alternatives are. Getting a second opinion before any extraction is reasonable.

## Retainers: the step that keeps the result

Every straightening treatment ends with a retainer, whether you had aligners or braces.

Teeth sit in bone, held by tiny fibers that act a bit like rubber bands. After teeth move, those fibers and the bone around them take time to settle, and the teeth tend to drift back toward where they started. On top of that, teeth keep shifting slowly throughout life. That's why adults who had braces as teens often notice crowding coming back.

A retainer holds teeth in their new spots. Options include a clear tray much like an aligner or a thin wire bonded behind the front teeth. We'll recommend the type that suits you and tell you how often to wear it. Plan on wearing a retainer for the long haul, often at night.

## Questions to ask before you choose

Bring these to any consultation, including ours. Does my concern come from health, looks, or both? Suppose I do nothing. What happens? Could bonding or another small fix get me most of what I want? Am I a good candidate for aligners, and if not, why not? Would you refer me to an orthodontist for this? Should any teeth come out, and what are the alternatives? And what will the retainer plan look like afterward?

If you're a parent, add one more. Honestly, will my kid wear the trays? You know the answer better than anyone.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Invisalign as effective as braces?

For mild to moderate crowding and spacing, aligners work well when worn as directed, while complex bite problems and large movements are often more predictable with braces.

### How young is too young for aligners?

Aligners made for teens allow for teeth still coming in. Readiness matters as much as age. At the exam, we look at your teen's teeth and talk about whether they're ready to keep the trays in.

### Can adults get braces or aligners?

Age isn't the limit. Healthy gums and bone matter more, so we check them before starting treatment.

### Do aligners hurt less than braces?

Both cause some pressure and soreness, mostly in the first days after a new tray or an adjustment, though aligners are usually gentler on the cheeks and lips, with no brackets to rub.

### I lost an aligner. Now what?

Call us right away, and we'll tell you whether to move to the next tray or replace the current one.

### Will I need a retainer after aligners too?

Yes, because teeth tend to drift after any kind of straightening.
