Take a typical case we see: a 34-year-old we'll call Maya. She had braces at 13, wore her retainer for a year or so, then lost it in a move. Twenty years later her lower front teeth have crowded back together. Two of them overlap, floss shreds when she works it between them, and she's started to hide her smile in photos. She asks the question this post is named for. Invisalign or braces?

Maya is made up. She's a blend of the adults who sit in our chair with this exact question, and we'll follow her (and, later, her son) through the decision because one person's story explains the trade-offs better than a chart does.

Some vocabulary first. Invisalign and braces are both orthodontic treatment, the branch of dentistry that moves teeth and lines up the bite. Invisalign is a brand of clear aligners: plastic trays you can take out. Braces are brackets and wire that stay on until the job is done. That's the real choice.

## Does Maya need treatment at all?

We ask this before anything else, and sometimes the honest answer is no.

Crooked teeth can cause real health problems. Crowded teeth are harder to brush and floss, so plaque builds up in the tight spots and raises the risk of cavities and gum disease. A bad bite, meaning the way your top and bottom teeth meet, can wear down enamel, chip edges, or strain the jaw joint. Any of those is a good reason to treat.

Other concerns are about looks. A small gap. One tooth that sits a little forward. A slight twist you only notice in photos. Those are fair things to care about, but they often have smaller fixes than full orthodontic treatment. If Maya had one small gap between her front teeth and an otherwise healthy bite, bonding might close it in a single visit: we shape tooth-colored resin onto the sides of the teeth, with little or no drilling. If her teeth had shifted a tiny bit and it didn't bother her, we'd keep an eye on it at her regular cleanings and leave it alone.

Her case is different. The overlap is making it hard to keep those teeth clean, and her gums there bleed when she flosses. Her problem has moved from cosmetic to health, and treatment makes sense.

We'll say this plainly, and some people won't like it: nobody should start orthodontics because a friend did or because an ad made it look easy. Start with an exam and a frank talk. Our post on [what conservative dentistry means](/blog/what-is-conservative-dentistry) explains how we think about this.

## How the two options move teeth

Both apply gentle, steady pressure that moves teeth a little at a time. The tools are what differ.

Aligners are thin, clear plastic trays made to fit your teeth. Planning starts with a digital scan of your mouth, and we map each small movement ahead of time. We shape each tray slightly differently from the one before, and on a set schedule you switch to the next one. Your teeth follow along. Some movements need something for the trays to grip, so we bond small tooth-colored bumps, called attachments, onto a few teeth. They're made of a resin like the one used in bonding, and we take them off at the end. Most people stop noticing them after a few days. There's more on our [Invisalign page](/services/orthodontic-services/invisalign).

Braces use small brackets glued to the front of each tooth, joined by a thin wire. The wire does the pushing and pulling, and at each visit the orthodontist adjusts or swaps it to keep teeth moving the right way. Rubber bands or other small parts can help correct how the jaws line up. Brackets come in metal or tooth color. You can't take them out.

## Why Maya got aligners and her son didn't

Aligners do best with mild to moderate problems. Braces handle a wider range, including the hard cases.

Maya fits the aligner profile well. Her crowding is mild, her bite is healthy, and she's an adult who had braces once and saw her teeth drift. Teeth that drift after braces are a common reason adults look into aligners. At her exam we'd check her gums and the bone around her teeth, since healthy support matters more than age, and treat the bleeding spot first if it needs it. Then we'd scan, plan, and talk through what the trays will ask of her.

Now her 14-year-old son, whom we'll call Theo. He comes along to her appointment and, since he's due for a checkup anyway, he gets one. Theo is a different story. His upper teeth sit well ahead of his lower ones (a large overbite), and his jaws are still growing. A couple of his teeth would also need to turn a long way, and turning rounder teeth is a movement aligners have a harder time with. Big vertical moves, like pulling a tooth down into place, fall in the same group.

Put together, those point to braces, and braces mean a referral. An orthodontist is a dentist with specialty training in straightening teeth and correcting bites. Our practice offers Invisalign, including Invisalign for Teens, but complex bites, jaw growth problems in kids, and cases that call for braces do better with a specialist. So we'd explain what we see and refer Theo to an orthodontist. We'd rather send someone out than keep them in the wrong treatment.

She earned her dental degree at Columbia University College of Dental Medicine and completed postgraduate training in orthodontics in India. Her training helps her plan aligner cases and spot the ones that need more than aligners can give.

There's a second reason Theo might be better off in braces, and it has nothing to do with his bite. Trays only work while they're in your mouth. Picture a teen who takes them out at lunch and leaves them in a backpack all afternoon. That teen won't get the result on the plan. Braces can't come out, which is a real advantage for a kid who forgets. No knock on teenagers, though. Adults forget too, and plenty of teens wear their trays better than their parents do.

Some cases fall in between. When that happens we lay out both paths and let you weigh them with us.

## The hours are the hard part

People underestimate this. Aligners need to be in your mouth 20 to 22 hours a day. That leaves two to four hours for eating, for drinking anything other than water, and for brushing.

Three meals and a couple of coffees use that up fast.

For Maya, the hard part won't be the plastic. It'll be the 3 p.m. latte and the long work lunch. If she forgets often, her teeth fall behind the plan, the next tray won't fit right, and treatment runs longer. Missing an hour now and then won't ruin anything. But steady wear is the single biggest factor in whether aligners work, and in our view it matters more than how carefully we plan the trays.

Teen sets from [Invisalign for Teens](/services/orthodontic-services/invisalign-for-teens) often include small wear indicators that fade with use, so parents (and we) can see whether the trays are staying in.

## Day to day, and keeping teeth healthy underneath

With aligners, Maya takes the trays out, eats what she likes, brushes, and puts them back. The one rule she'll be tempted to bend is sipping coffee with sugar, juice, or soda with the trays in. Plastic trays hold whatever is on your teeth right against them, so a sweet drink sits on the enamel for hours. Please don't. Rinse the trays when they come out, and brush before they go back in.

Theo's list is about food. Hard things like nuts, popcorn, and ice can break a bracket. Caramel and other sticky foods get stuck in the wire. Cleaning takes longer too, because brackets create small ledges where plaque collects. Left there, it can leave chalky white spots on the enamel, an early sign of mineral loss that can stay visible after the braces come off. Careful brushing around every bracket and cleaning under the wire, with a floss threader or a water flosser, keeps those spots from forming.

Comfort is different for each of them. Maya may have a slight lisp for the first few days, which usually fades as her tongue gets used to the trays, and each new tray feels tight for a day or two. Theo's brackets will probably rub the inside of his cheeks and lips early on; a bit of orthodontic wax over the bracket helps. Soreness for a few days after each wire adjustment is normal.

He plays soccer, so he'll need an orthodontic mouthguard, since a hit to the mouth can cut the lips on the brackets. Maya can usually take her trays out for a game or wear a guard, whichever we advise.

Gums matter for both. Puffy, bleeding gums slow treatment down and make it less comfortable, which is one more reason we'd sort out Maya's sore spot before she starts. Keep up regular cleanings and exams the whole way through. We'd rather catch a problem spot early than find it on the day you finish. Straight teeth with new cavities aren't a good trade.

Both options take months, and the length depends on the teeth. Maya will get her trays in sets and come in for check-ins so we can confirm her teeth are tracking; sometimes a plan needs a small correction partway, so we rescan and make a few extra trays. Theo will see his orthodontist regularly for wire adjustments, and skipping those visits slows everything down. We won't quote a timeline before we've looked at your teeth, because any number before then would be a guess.

## About pulling teeth

Some orthodontic plans call for removing healthy teeth to make room. With severe crowding that can be the right call. Our preference is to avoid it when we can, by gently widening the arch or by removing a thin sliver of enamel between some teeth, a step called interproximal reduction. Interproximal reduction does take away a little enamel, so we only suggest it when the trade makes sense, and we explain it first. If someone recommends taking out healthy teeth for you or your child, ask why and what the alternatives are. A second opinion before any extraction is reasonable.

## The retainer is where Maya went wrong the first time

Remember how her story started. The braces worked. The lost retainer is why she's back.

Teeth sit in bone, held by tiny fibers that act a bit like rubber bands. After teeth move, those fibers and the bone around them take time to settle, and the teeth tend to drift back toward where they started. Teeth also keep shifting slowly throughout life, aligners or not. It's also why adults who had braces as teens often notice crowding coming back in their thirties.

A retainer holds teeth in their new spots. It might be a clear tray much like an aligner, or a thin wire bonded behind the front teeth. We'll recommend the type that suits you and tell you how often to wear it. Plan on wearing one for the long haul, often at night. Honestly, it's the least exciting part of the whole process, and the part we'd ask you to take most seriously. Theo will get the same speech from his orthodontist.
