Say you just moved to Redmond for a new job. The boxes are mostly unpacked, school starts next week, and your son chipped a tooth on moving day. Now you need a dentist and have no idea who to call.

This guide is for that moment. People who have lived on the Eastside for years and want to switch can use it too. We run a dental practice in Bellevue, so yes, we have a stake here. But everything below applies to any office you are considering, including ours.

## Start with how the dentist recommends treatment

Learn how a dentist decides what you need before anything else. Skill matters, of course. Still, nearly every licensed dentist can place a good filling, and what differs from office to office is judgment.

Good dentists start with the least invasive option, meaning the one that removes the least tooth and costs you the least time and money. They tell you when something can be watched instead of fixed today. Take early decay on the surface of a tooth, which sometimes stays stable for years with better brushing and fluoride. Hearing "let's check this again in six months" is a sign of honesty about what the tooth needs.

Ask why. If a dentist recommends a crown (a cap that covers the whole tooth), expect to hear what is wrong with the tooth, what happens if you wait, and what your other choices are. Try repeating the reason back in your own words. Can't? Push for a clearer answer.

We rank this above price and location. Over ten years, a convenient dentist who over-treats will cost you more than a slightly farther one who doesn't.

## Questions to ask when you call

The phone call tells you more than the website does. Front-desk staff answer the same questions all day, and how they handle yours previews how the whole office runs.

Find out whether they are taking new patients and how soon they can see you. What does the first visit include, and how long does it take? Parents will want to know what age the office starts seeing children and whether the whole family can come in on the same day. Also ask what happens when a current patient has an emergency, like a broken tooth or sudden pain, and how fast they can get in.

Payment and insurance come next, covered below. Meanwhile, notice whether the person on the phone gives you clear answers or says "we'll figure it out when you come in."

One more question: how long have the dentist and hygienists worked there? High turnover changes how an office feels, and you end up re-explaining your history to a new face every visit.

## What a good first visit includes

Expect the first visit to cover your whole mouth, not just the tooth that hurts, and to run longer than a routine checkup.

First comes a full exam, where the dentist checks every tooth, your bite, your jaw, and the soft tissue inside your mouth, which doubles as an oral cancer screening and is barely noticeable.

The dentist takes X-rays as needed. Had a set taken at your last dentist recently? Then have them sent over, and the new office often won't need more. Any decent office asks about this instead of taking a full set by default.

Your hygienist also checks your gums, using a small ruler to measure the pockets between your gums and teeth. Deep pockets point to gum disease, an infection of the tissue and bone that hold your teeth in place. Those numbers are among the most useful things you leave with, so ask what yours were.

Next comes a cleaning, assuming your gums are healthy enough for a standard one. Some patients need a deeper cleaning first, and the office owes you an explanation before doing it.

Last is a conversation, where the dentist sits down with you, goes over the findings, and sorts them into urgent, can-wait, and fine. Here is where you learn how they recommend treatment. Do they ask about your goals and your budget, or just hand you a printout?

## Red flags to watch for

Warning signs are easy to spot once you know them.

The first flag is pressure to commit to a large treatment plan on the day you walk in. Real dental problems rarely get worse in the week it takes you to think it over, and a dentist who is confident in the plan will give you that week.

Missing explanations are the second flag. "You need four fillings" explains nothing. "These two show decay on the X-ray, and these two are early spots we can watch" does.

Defensiveness about a second opinion is the third. Getting one is normal for big work like implants, multiple crowns, or braces. Confident dentists will send your X-rays to another office if you ask.

And honestly, trust your gut about how the chair felt. Felt rushed, talked over, or embarrassed about your teeth? Six months later you'll feel it again. Nobody should make you feel bad for the years you skipped the dentist. Plenty of people fall behind, especially after a move.

## Kids' first visits and care for every age

Bring children in by their first birthday or within six months of their first tooth. That visit is short. Its purpose is to get a toddler used to the chair and give parents tips on brushing, bottles, and teething.

With older kids, look for a team that goes at the child's pace. Picture a nervous six-year-old. The right hygienist shows them the tools, lets them hold the mirror, and skips anything that isn't needed that day. Early visits shape how your kid feels about dentists as an adult.

Family practices see all ages under one roof. Your toddler, your teenager, you, and your parents can all go to the same office. Records stay in one place, and one team learns your family's history. So when your daughter's teeth start coming in crowded, the dentist already knows her dad needed braces at the same age.

Older adults have their own needs. Dry mouth from medications, worn teeth, aging fillings, and receding gums are common after 60. Seniors, ask whether the practice regularly sees older patients and whether it coordinates with your doctor when your health or medications affect dental care.

## Insurance and payment questions to ask

Every plan is different, and calling the office is the only way to know how yours works there.

Keep your insurance card handy. Ask whether the office is in-network with your plan, which means it has a pricing agreement with your insurer and usually lowers what you pay. Will they bill your insurance directly, or do you pay up front and get reimbursed later?

Request a rough cost for your first visit after insurance. Exact numbers aren't always possible, but a range is. Before bigger work, ask whether they will send a pre-estimate, a request to your insurer asking what it will cover, so the bill doesn't surprise you.

Without insurance, ask what payment options exist, such as payment plans or in-house membership plans. Front desks hear that question daily.

## In-house care or a specialist referral?

By in-house care, we mean the dentist does the work in their own office instead of sending you elsewhere. Offices draw this line in different places.

General dentists handle most routine needs, such as cleanings, fillings, crowns, and simple extractions. Some general dentists also do root canals (treatment that removes an infected nerve from inside a tooth), whitening, and other cosmetic work. Having one team handle more of your care means fewer new offices, fewer forms, and a dentist who already knows your mouth.

Referrals are the right call for complicated work. A complex root canal on a back molar, wisdom teeth lying sideways in the jaw, or advanced gum disease often goes better with a specialist who does that work all day. Put the question to any new dentist: what do they do themselves, and what do they send out? Knowing their limits is a point in their favor.

## Logistics for Eastside families

Pick an office you can reach without a fight. An hour of traffic plus a hunt for parking means you'll put off cleanings, and small problems grow into big ones.

Think about where you spend your days. A Sammamish family with a parent working downtown has different options than a Kirkland family who works from home. For much of the Eastside, the main routes between Bellevue and Redmond (148th Ave NE, NE Bel-Red Rd, and SR-520) cover a lot of ground, so an office near one of them is an easy stop from home, work, or school.

Parking matters more than people expect, especially with small kids in car seats. Free parking by the door beats a paid garage three floors down.

Hours are next. Working parents like early morning appointments, since they can get in and out before work. Kids who finish school around 3 do better with early afternoon slots. ## Switching dentists and transferring your records

Switching is easier than people think, and you owe your old dentist no explanation.

Schedule with the new office first. After that, ask your old office to send your records, including recent X-rays, charts, and notes on past treatment. You'll sign a short release form, and the new office can request the records for you if you give them the old office's name and number.

Get the records sent before your first visit, not after. That way the new dentist can compare old X-rays with new ones, spot changes, and sometimes skip repeat X-rays altogether.

Leaving because of a bad experience? Tell the new dentist what happened, since it helps them understand what matters to you.

## How to read reviews critically

Reviews help, but a star rating alone tells you little.

Look for detail. "Great office!" is nice and useless. Contrast that with a review saying the hygienist noticed a problem, explained it, and did not push treatment tells you something real. Long-term patients are worth extra weight, since they have seen the office on good days and bad ones.

Read the negative reviews too. Billing mix-ups and long waits happen everywhere. Patterns are different. Three or four people separately saying they felt pressured into expensive work is a pattern worth believing over any single angry post.

Watch how the office responds. One calm, specific reply to a complaint says more than ten five-star reviews.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often does my family need a checkup?

Most people do well with a checkup and cleaning every six months. Patients with gum disease often need cleanings every three or four months. Your dentist will tell you which schedule fits.

### Will I need new X-rays if I just had them at my old dentist?

Often not. Send the old ones over, and the new dentist will check their age and quality before deciding.

### Can my kids and I be seen on the same day?

Back-to-back appointments are common at family practices. Timing matters, since those blocks fill up around school breaks, so ask early.

### Haven't been to a dentist in years?

Book the visit anyway. Expect no lecture from a good office. They'll look at where things stand today and help you make a plan.

### Is it rude to get a second opinion?

No. Second opinions are a normal part of health care, and good dentists expect them for large treatment plans.
