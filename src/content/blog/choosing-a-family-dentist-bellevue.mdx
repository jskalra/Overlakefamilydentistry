Picture a family we'll call the Nguyens. They aren't real, but we meet families like them all the time. They just moved to Redmond for a new job. The boxes are mostly unpacked, school starts next week, and their eight-year-old, Leo, chipped a front tooth on moving day. His little sister, Mai, is three and has never seen a dentist. Grandma lives with them now too. And nobody in the house knows who to call.

We'll follow the Nguyens from the first phone call through their first few visits. If you've lived here for years and just want to switch, this works for you too. We run a dental practice in Bellevue, so yes, we have a stake here. Everything below still applies to any office you're looking at, including ours.

## Start with how a dentist decides what you need

Before hours, before parking, before reviews, find out how a dentist makes recommendations. Nearly every licensed dentist can place a good filling. What changes from office to office is judgment.

Good dentists start with the least invasive option, the one that removes the least tooth and costs you the least time and money. They'll tell you when something can be watched instead of fixed today. Early decay on the surface of a tooth, for example, sometimes stays stable for years with better brushing and fluoride. "Let's check this again in six months" can be the most honest thing a dentist says.

So ask why. If a dentist suggests a crown (a cap that covers the whole tooth), you should hear what's wrong with the tooth, what happens if you wait, and what else you could do. Try repeating the reason back in your own words. If you can't, ask again until you can.

We'd rank this above price and above a short drive. Some people will disagree, and we get it, because a close office is easier to keep going to. But over ten years, a convenient dentist who over-treats will cost you more than a slightly farther one who doesn't.

## The first phone call

Mr. Nguyen calls three offices on a Monday morning. That call tells him more than any website.

He asks whether they're taking new patients and how soon Leo can be seen for the chipped tooth. He asks what a first visit includes and how long it lasts, and what age they start seeing kids, since Mai is three. Can the family come in on the same day, one after another? What happens when a current patient breaks a tooth or wakes up in pain on a Tuesday?

Then a question people forget: how long have the dentist and hygienists worked there? If staff turn over every year, you tell your history to a new face at every cleaning.

He's listening for clear answers. One office says "we'll figure all that out when you come in." It goes to the bottom of his list.

## What the first visit should cover

A week later, Mrs. Nguyen goes in first. A good first visit looks at your whole mouth, not just the tooth that bothers you, and it runs longer than a routine checkup.

It starts with a full exam. The dentist checks every tooth, the bite, the jaw, and the soft tissue inside the mouth. That last part doubles as an oral cancer screening, and you'll barely notice it.

X-rays come next, if they're needed. Mrs. Nguyen had a set taken at her old dentist in Ohio last spring, so she asked that office to send them, and the new office may not need to take more. A decent office asks about this instead of taking a full set by default.

The hygienist checks her gums with a tiny ruler, measuring the pockets between gums and teeth. Deep pockets point to gum disease, an infection of the tissue and bone that hold teeth in place. Those numbers are some of the most useful things you'll leave with. Ask what yours were. It's an easy question to forget.

If her gums are healthy, she gets a regular cleaning. Some people need a deeper cleaning first, and the office should explain why before starting.

Last comes a talk. The dentist sits down, goes over what they found, and sorts it into urgent, can wait, and fine. This is where you see how they recommend treatment. Do they ask about your goals and budget? Or do they hand you a printout and leave? Mrs. Nguyen walks out knowing she has one small cavity to fix and two spots to watch. That's a good first visit.

## Leo, Mai, and Grandma

Leo's chipped tooth turns out to be minor. A small bonding repair, tooth-colored material shaped onto the edge, and he's done. What mattered more was how the hygienist treated a nervous eight-year-old. She showed him the tools, let him hold the mirror, and skipped anything that didn't need doing that day. Early visits like that shape how a kid feels about dentists for the rest of their life, which is a lot to ask of one short appointment.

Mai's visit is shorter still. Kids should come in by their first birthday or within six months of their first tooth, so at three she's a little late, and that's fine. The goal is to get her used to the chair and give her parents tips on brushing and snacks. Nobody should make a parent feel bad about timing.

Grandma has her own needs. After 60, dry mouth from medications, worn teeth, aging fillings, and receding gums are all common. If someone in your family is older, ask whether the practice regularly sees older patients and whether it will work with their doctor when health problems or medications affect dental care.

This is the case for a family practice. Toddler, teenager, parents, and grandparents in one office, with one set of records and one team that learns the family's history. When Mai's teeth start coming in crowded someday, the dentist will already know her dad needed braces at the same age.

One more thing to ask: what does the office do itself, and what does it send out? General dentists handle cleanings, fillings, crowns, and simple extractions. Some also do root canals (removing an infected nerve from inside a tooth) and cosmetic work. Wisdom teeth lying sideways in the jaw, a tricky root canal on a back molar, or advanced gum disease often go better with a specialist who does that work all day. We think a dentist who knows when to refer is worth more than one who says yes to everything.

## Insurance, without the guesswork

Plans differ, and only the office can tell you how yours works there.

Mr. Nguyen keeps his card in hand and asks three things. Is the office in-network with his plan, meaning it has a pricing agreement with his insurer? Will they bill the insurer directly? And before any bigger work, will they send a pre-estimate, a request asking the insurer what it will cover? If you don't have insurance, ask what the first visit will cost before you book it.

## A red flag they might hit somewhere else

Say one of the offices on Mr. Nguyen's list had seen him first. In one short visit he hears that he needs six fillings and two crowns, and the front desk wants to book it all before he leaves. Nobody shows him which teeth or why. When he asks about a second opinion, the mood in the room changes.

Each of those is a warning sign.

Real dental problems rarely get worse in the week it takes to think a plan over, and a dentist who's sure of the plan will give you that week. "You need four fillings" explains nothing. "These two show decay on the X-ray, and these two are early spots we can watch" does. Second opinions are normal for big work like implants, several crowns, or braces, and a confident dentist will send your X-rays to another office if you ask.

Then there's the feeling in the chair. If you felt rushed, talked over, or embarrassed about your teeth, you'll feel it again in six months. Lots of people fall behind on dental visits, especially after a move. No one should make you feel bad about that.

Online reviews can help you spot this before you book, if you read them closely. A star rating alone tells you little, and "Great office!" is nice but useless. A review that says the hygienist found a problem, explained it, and didn't push treatment tells you something real. Long-time patients deserve extra weight, since they've seen the office on good days and bad. Read the bad reviews too. Billing mix-ups and long waits happen everywhere. But three or four people saying, separately, that they felt pushed into expensive work is a pattern worth believing. Honestly, one calm, specific reply from the office to a complaint says more than ten five-star reviews.

## Getting there, and moving your records

Pick an office you can reach without a fight. If it means an hour of traffic and a hunt for parking, you'll put off cleanings, and small problems will grow.

Think about where you spend your days. A Sammamish family with a parent working downtown has different choices than a Kirkland family who works from home. For the Nguyens, the main routes between Redmond and Bellevue (148th Ave NE, NE Bel-Red Rd., and SR-520) make an office near one of them an easy stop from home, work, or school. Parking matters more than people expect when you're unbuckling a three-year-old from a car seat. Free spots near the door beat a paid garage three floors down.

Hours count too. Working parents like early-morning slots so they can get in and out before work. Kids who finish school around 3 do better in the early afternoon. Ask how far out cleanings are booked and whether the office keeps time open for emergencies.

Switching is easier than people think, and you don't owe your old dentist an explanation. Book with the new office first. Then ask the old one to send your records, including recent X-rays, charts, and notes on past work. You'll sign a short release, and the new office can request the records for you if you give them the old office's name and number. Try to get them sent before the first visit so the new dentist can compare old X-rays with new ones. And if you're leaving because of a bad experience, tell the new dentist what happened. It helps them understand what matters to you.
