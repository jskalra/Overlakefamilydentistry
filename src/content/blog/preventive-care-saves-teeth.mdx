Most dental problems start small and quiet. That is the whole case for preventive care. Early decay or puffy gums rarely hurt, so you won't know they're there unless someone looks. Caught early, those problems need a small fix. Caught late, they can mean a crown, a root canal, or losing the tooth.

For more than 30 years we've cared for Eastside families, and our approach is conservative: keep as much of your natural tooth as we can, for as long as we can. Regular checkups are how we do that.

## How a small problem turns into a big one

Cavities and gum disease get harder to treat the longer they go. Each stage below takes more work to fix than the one before it.

### A cavity, stage by stage

Decay starts in the enamel, the hard white outer shell of the tooth. Bacteria in plaque (the sticky film that builds up on teeth every day) feed on sugar and make acid, and that acid softens enamel. You might see a chalky white spot. Or nothing at all. Sometimes fluoride and better home care stop it right there, with no drilling.

Left alone, decay breaks through into the dentin, the softer, yellowish layer under the enamel. Decay spreads through dentin faster. This is usually when people start to notice something. Say you feel a twinge when you sip ice water, or a sweet snack makes one spot ache for a second. Our usual fix here is a filling: we remove the decayed part and seal the space.

Past the dentin sits the pulp, the soft center of the tooth that holds the nerve and blood supply. Once bacteria reach it, the tooth can hurt badly, or it can die quietly and form an abscess (a pocket of infection at the root tip). Now you're looking at a root canal, which cleans out the inside of the tooth, usually followed by a crown to protect what's left. When too much tooth has broken down, pulling it may be the only choice, and then you're deciding between a bridge, an implant, or a gap.

Same tooth. Same cavity. Only the timing changed.

### Gum disease, stage by stage

Gum disease follows a similar path. It begins as gingivitis, which is inflammation of the gums caused by plaque along the gumline. Your gums may look red or swollen and bleed when you brush or floss. Gingivitis is reversible, and a professional cleaning plus steady brushing and flossing usually clears it up.

Plaque that stays on teeth long enough hardens into tartar (also called calculus), a crusty deposit a toothbrush can't remove. Tartar gives bacteria a home right against the gums. Over time the infection can move below the gumline and damage the bone and tissue that hold your teeth in place. That's periodontitis. Gums pull away from the teeth and form pockets, the bone wears away, and teeth can loosen.

For early periodontitis, we usually recommend a deep cleaning, called scaling and root planing. Your hygienist cleans below the gumline and smooths the root surfaces so the gums can heal against them. Advanced cases may need more frequent maintenance cleanings or a referral to a gum specialist. Bone lost to periodontitis doesn't grow back on its own, which is why we'd much rather catch gums at the bleeding stage.

## What happens at a checkup and cleaning

A preventive visit has two parts, a cleaning by the hygienist and an exam by the dentist, in one appointment.

Your hygienist goes first and asks about changes in your health and medications, since certain medicines affect your mouth. Tartar comes off with small hand tools or an ultrasonic scaler, a tool that uses vibration and water to break deposits loose. Then come flossing and a polish with gritty paste to lift stains. Your hygienist also measures the space between each tooth and the gum with a thin probe marked like a tiny ruler. Healthy gums have shallow spaces. Deeper readings, or spots that bleed, point to gum disease.

Dr. Ravneet Kaur, who leads our practice, then does the exam. She looks at each tooth for decay, cracks, worn spots, and loose or failing fillings, checks your bite and jaw joints, and screens your cheeks, tongue, and throat for signs of oral cancer. We compare what we see with your last visit. Small changes stand out when there's a record to compare against, and that's where regular visits pay off.

If we find something, we'll show you, explain your options, and tell you straight whether it needs treatment now or can be watched. Watching is often the right call.

### Why we take X-rays, and how often

X-rays show what eyes can't: decay between teeth where they touch, problems under old fillings, bone levels around the roots, and infections at root tips.

We don't put everyone on the same X-ray calendar. Your age, your history of cavities, the health of your gums, and any symptoms decide how often you need them. Someone who hasn't had a cavity in years needs bitewings (the small X-rays that show the back teeth) less often than someone who gets new cavities every year. Digital X-rays use a low dose of radiation, and we only take the ones that will change a decision.

## Home care that works

What you do every day matters more than what we do twice a year.

### Brushing

Brush twice a day for two full minutes. Two minutes feels long and it's easy to quit early, so use a timer or an electric brush with one built in. Pick soft bristles. Hard bristles and heavy pressure wear down gums and enamel without cleaning any better.

Tilt the bristles toward the gumline at about a 45-degree angle and use small, gentle circles or short strokes. Cover the outside, inside, and chewing surfaces of every tooth; the inner sides of the lower front teeth and the backs of the last molars are the spots people miss most.

Use a toothpaste with fluoride, a mineral that strengthens enamel and helps repair the earliest stage of decay. After brushing, spit, but skip the rinse. Leaving a thin film of toothpaste behind gives the fluoride more time to work.

### Cleaning between your teeth

Your toothbrush can't reach the tight spaces between teeth, and that's where cavities and gum problems often start. Clean between your teeth once a day.

Floss works when you use it right. Curve it into a C shape around each tooth, slide it gently just under the gumline, and move it up and down. Snapping it straight down and back out doesn't do much. Struggle with floss? That's fine. Floss picks, interdental brushes (tiny bottle-brushes that fit between teeth), and water flossers are all reasonable options. Our view: the best tool is the one you'll use every night. Honestly, a floss pick you grab daily beats a roll of floss sitting in the drawer.

### Snacking and sugary drinks

How often you eat sugar matters more than how much. Every time you eat or drink something sugary, plaque bacteria make acid for a while afterward. Saliva then neutralizes that acid and helps enamel recover. Sip a sweet coffee drink or a soda over a whole afternoon, and your teeth never get that recovery time.

So have dessert with a meal instead of grazing on it later. Water is the best drink between meals. Sports drinks, juice, and sweetened teas all count as sugary drinks, even when they seem healthy. Sticky snacks like dried fruit and gummy candy cling to teeth and keep acid going longer.

## Prevention for kids

Children should see a dentist by their first birthday, or within six months of their first tooth coming in. That visit is short. We look at the teeth, check for early problems, and talk with parents about brushing, bottles, and fluoride. Kids also get used to the chair before anything needs fixing, which makes later visits easier.

Sealants are among the best tools we have for kids. A sealant is a thin plastic coating painted onto the chewing surfaces of the back teeth, where deep grooves trap food and are hard to brush clean. Placing one is quick, involves no drilling or numbing, and keeps decay out of those grooves. We usually recommend them as the permanent molars come in, around age 6 and again around age 12.

Fluoride varnish, a concentrated coating brushed on at checkups, gives young enamel extra protection. At home, use a smear of fluoride toothpaste the size of a grain of rice for children under 3, and a pea-sized amount from age 3 on. Help your child brush until they can do it well alone, which usually happens later than kids think.

## Prevention for adults and seniors

Your mouth changes as you age, and the risks shift with it. We see patients from toddlers to seniors, and in older adults three problems come up more than any others.

Dry mouth is a big one. Saliva washes away food and neutralizes acid, so less saliva means more decay. Common medications cause dry mouth, including some for blood pressure, allergies, depression, and pain. Tell us and your doctor if your mouth often feels dry. Sipping water, chewing sugar-free gum, and using a prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste can help.

Gum recession is another. As gums pull back, they expose the roots of the teeth. Roots have no enamel, so they decay more easily and can be sensitive to cold. Gentle brushing with a soft brush keeps recession from getting worse, and fluoride helps protect exposed roots.

Older fillings and crowns wear out, too. Decades-old fillings can leak or crack at the edges, and decay can grow underneath where you can't see it. With regular exams and X-rays, we can replace a failing filling at a planned visit instead of fixing a broken tooth in an emergency.

### Nightguards for grinding

People often grind or clench their teeth at night without knowing it. Watch for a sore jaw or a headache when you wake up, teeth that look flat or chipped, or a partner who hears the grinding. Over years, grinding wears down enamel, cracks teeth, and breaks fillings.

Custom nightguards are thin plastic trays, made from a mold of your teeth, that you wear while you sleep. The guard takes the force so your teeth don't. We count it as prevention, because a nightguard costs far less, in money and in time in the chair, than the crowns that years of heavy grinding can require.

## What about cost and insurance?

Prevention is almost always the cheaper path. An exam and cleaning costs less than a filling, and a filling costs less than a root canal and crown. Keeping a tooth costs less than replacing one. You also spend less time in the chair and less time numb.

Many plans cover preventive visits, such as exams, cleanings, and routine X-rays; check your plan for details like how many cleanings a year it includes. We accept most dental insurance plans and file claims on your behalf, so you don't have to deal with the paperwork.

## Signs you shouldn't wait for your next checkup

Don't sit on a problem until your next scheduled visit. Call us if you have a toothache that lasts more than a day or two, or pain that wakes you at night. Swelling in your face or gums, especially with a bad taste or a fever, needs attention the same day. Other reasons to call sooner: gums that bleed every time you brush, a tooth that feels loose, a chipped or broken tooth or filling, sensitivity to hot or cold that lingers after the drink is gone, a mouth sore that hasn't healed in two weeks, or jaw pain that makes chewing hard.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should I get a cleaning?

Every six months works well for most people. Patients with a history of gum disease often do better with visits every three or four months. We'll recommend a schedule based on what we see in your mouth, not a one-size rule.

### My teeth don't hurt. Do I still need a checkup?

Yes. Early cavities and early gum disease usually don't hurt. By the time a tooth aches, the decay has often reached the nerve. Checkups catch problems during the painless stage, when they're easy to fix.

### Why do my gums bleed when I floss?

Usually because they're inflamed from plaque, the early stage of gingivitis. It seems backwards, but the fix is to keep flossing gently every day. In most people the bleeding settles down within a week or two. Let us know if it doesn't.

### Is it too late for prevention if I already have problems?

Not at all. Even if you need fillings or gum treatment now, good home care and regular visits keep new problems from starting and help the work you've had done last longer.
