Candy canes are hard on teeth, and not mostly because of the sugar. The trouble is that people bite them.

A candy cane is hard, brittle sugar, and a back tooth with an old filling has edges that crack under that kind of load. So does peanut brittle, and so do the unpopped kernels at the bottom of the popcorn bowl. The holidays add a few other risks too: grazing on sweets all afternoon, travel far from your usual dentist, and a calendar so full that checkups slip into next year. None of this means you should skip the cookies. It means knowing which habits matter, and what to do if something goes wrong on a Saturday.

## Treats, timing, and the eggnog problem

Here's the part most people get backward. The amount of sugar you eat matters less than how long it sits on your teeth. Every time you eat or sip something sweet, the bacteria in plaque turn that sugar into acid, and the acid softens enamel for a while afterward. Saliva then neutralizes it and slowly repairs the surface. One slice of pie after dinner gives your teeth one acid attack. A tin of cookies on the counter that you visit every hour gives them six, with no recovery time in between.

That's why we'd rather you eat dessert with the meal than skip it and snack later. Some people will disagree, and that's fine. Slow, all-day eating is harder on teeth than one big holiday dinner.

Drinks work the same way. Eggnog, hot cider, spiced lattes, and sweet cocktails are easy to sip for two hours at a party, and two hours of sipping is two hours of acid. Wine and citrus add acid of their own. If you want the drink, have it, then switch to water. Water rinses sugar off your teeth and keeps your mouth from drying out, which matters more than you'd think after a night of salty food and alcohol.

Some treats are harder on teeth than others:

| Treat | Why it's a problem | A better way |
|---|---|---|
| Caramels, toffee, taffy, dried fruit | Sticky, so sugar stays in the grooves of back teeth for a long time | Eat them with a meal, then rinse with water |
| Candy canes, peanut brittle, hard candy | Hard enough to crack teeth and pull out fillings | Let them dissolve, don't bite down |
| Sweet drinks sipped slowly | Long exposure, many small acid attacks | Finish the drink, then drink water |
| Citrus, wine, cranberry sauce | Acidic, softens enamel on contact | Wait about 30 minutes before brushing |

That last row surprises people. Brushing right after something acidic can scrub at enamel while it's soft. Rinse with water first, wait half an hour, then brush. Cheese, nuts, and raw vegetables are kind to teeth, so a cheese plate is a good place to end up at a party.

## Your teeth are not a nutcracker

Bottle caps, tape on a gift box, the plastic tag on a new sweater. Teeth are for chewing food. Using them as a tool is an easy way to chip a front tooth, and the holidays hand you a lot of packages.

A small crack doesn't always hurt at first. Watch for a sharp pain when you bite and release, or a tooth that suddenly reacts to cold. Those are reasons to call, even if the pain goes away.

## When a tooth breaks on a Saturday

Our office is open Monday through Thursday, and dental problems don't keep to that schedule. Here's how one of them might go. This is a made-up example, not a real patient.

Say a woman we'll call Nancy is at a family party on a Saturday evening. She bites into a piece of peanut brittle and feels something give. A corner of a lower back tooth, one with an old filling, has broken off. It doesn't hurt much, but the edge is sharp against her tongue.

What should she do? First, keep the piece if she can find it, and rinse her mouth gently with warm water. If the sharp edge is cutting her tongue or cheek, a little sugar-free gum or dental wax from a pharmacy can cover it. She should chew on the other side and skip anything very hot, very cold, or hard until she's seen. An over-the-counter pain reliever, taken as the label directs, helps if the tooth starts to ache. She shouldn't put aspirin directly on the gum. It burns the tissue and doesn't help the tooth.

Then she calls. For patients who can't reach the front desk, our dental emergency line takes calls and texts at (571) 341-0957. A broken corner like Nancy's usually isn't an emergency in the middle-of-the-night sense, but it does need a visit soon. A chipped tooth with no pain can often be repaired with bonding (tooth-colored resin) or, if a lot of the tooth is gone, it may need a crown. Leaving it for weeks is how a small break turns into a bigger one.

Some problems can't wait. Swelling in the face or jaw, especially with a fever, or trouble swallowing or breathing, means urgent care or the emergency room right away. A dental infection can spread, and that's not something to manage over a holiday weekend.

## If it happens while you're traveling

Plenty of Eastside families spend the holidays somewhere else, which means being far from your own dentist when something breaks. A few situations and what to do:

A knocked-out adult tooth is the real emergency. Pick it up by the top part you chew with, not the root. If it's dirty, rinse it briefly with milk or water without scrubbing. If you can, gently push it back into the socket and bite on a clean cloth to hold it. If that isn't possible, keep it in a small container of milk and get to a dentist as fast as you can, within 30 minutes if possible. The sooner a tooth goes back in, the better its chances. (Baby teeth are different. Don't put a knocked-out baby tooth back in. Just call.)

A crown that comes off is less urgent. Keep it somewhere safe. Pharmacies sell temporary dental cement that can hold it in place for a few days, but don't use household glue. Then see a dentist, here or wherever you are.

A toothache that won't quit? Rinse with warm salt water, floss gently in case food is stuck, and use a cold compress on the outside of your cheek. If it keeps getting worse, or swelling starts, find a local dentist rather than waiting until you're home.

And pack the small things. People remember the toothbrush and forget the night guard, the retainer, or the aligner case. A week without a retainer is enough for teeth to start shifting back, and a week without a night guard can mean a sore jaw by New Year's.

## Kids, stockings, and sledding

Advent calendars are a tricky one. A small chocolate every morning before school isn't much sugar, but it's a daily snack on its own, outside a meal. Moving it to after dinner, once the plate is cleared, makes it one fewer acid attack a day. Sticky gummies and sour candies are harder on kids' teeth than plain chocolate, which melts and rinses away faster.

A new toothbrush in the stocking is a cliché for a reason. Kids who pick their own brush, in a color or character they like, tend to argue less at bedtime. Fluoride toothpaste matters more than the brush itself: a smear the size of a grain of rice for children under three, and a pea-sized amount after that.

Winter brings sledding, skating, and hockey, and a fall on the ice can chip a child's front tooth. If your child plays hockey or any contact sport, a fitted mouthguard is worth having. We make custom sports guards, and we're happy to talk about what kind fits your child's sport.

## Whitening before the holiday photos

Thinking about whitening before the family photos? Start early. A couple of days is too short for most methods. Take-home whitening with trays works over a couple of weeks, so starting in early December gives you time to see results and to stop if your teeth get sensitive.

Get a checkup first. Whitening gel on a tooth with a cavity, a cracked filling, or receding gums can hurt, and it won't fix any of those things. Whitening also only changes natural enamel. Crowns, veneers, and tooth-colored fillings stay the color they are, so if you have one on a front tooth, it may look darker next to whitened teeth. That's worth knowing before you start, not after.

Sensitivity is the most common side effect. It's usually mild and goes away when you stop. Wearing trays for less time each day, or skipping a day, often helps. Store-bought strips use a one-size shape that doesn't match every mouth, which is one reason gel can end up on the gums. Trays made from a scan of your own teeth keep the gel where it belongs.

## One habit to take into January

If you only change one thing this season, make it this: finish the treat, then drink a glass of water. It costs nothing, it works at any party, and it cuts the time sugar and acid sit on your teeth. The cookies are part of the holidays. We'd just like your teeth to make it to January in one piece.
