Conservative dentistry means keeping as much of your natural tooth as we can, for as long as we can. We treat what needs treating, pick the smallest fix that solves the problem, and tell you plainly when the right move is to wait.

That sounds obvious. Not every office works this way, though, and the difference shows up years later in how many of your own teeth you still have.

## Your teeth don't grow back

Once tooth structure is gone, it stays gone. Skin heals after a cut on your finger, but your body can't rebuild enamel, the hard outer layer of the tooth. Dentin doesn't regrow either (that's the softer, yellowish layer under the enamel).

So every time a dentist drills, the tooth gets a little smaller for good. Fillings replace the missing part with a man-made material. Modern materials do a fine job, but none of them bond to the tooth as well as the original did, and at some point the filling will need to be redone. Every redo takes a bit more tooth with it.

This is the core idea behind conservative care. You only get one set of adult teeth, and we plan your treatment with that in mind.

## What "conservative" looks like in the chair

In practice, we recommend work only when there's a clear reason for it, and we reach for the least invasive option that will hold up.

Say you come in and we spot a small dark area on the chewing surface of a molar. That spot could be early decay. Or it could be a stain in a groove that has looked the same for years. When the enamel is still hard and the X-ray shows nothing underneath, we note it, take a photo, and check it again at your next cleaning. No drilling that day.

Once a spot has gone past the enamel, or it grows between visits, we treat it. Waiting on real decay makes the eventual repair bigger, which defeats the point.

Doing the right-sized thing at the right time is the goal. Sometimes that's nothing yet. Other times it's a root canal.

## Your options, side by side

Here's the plain version: for most problems there's a range of fixes, from small to large. We'll show you where your tooth sits on that range and why.

### Watch-and-wait, a small filling, an onlay, or a crown

These four options run from least to most tooth removed.

Watch-and-wait is for early spots that haven't broken through the enamel. Along with the follow-up, we can add a fluoride treatment or suggest a change in how you brush or floss that area, then look again in six months. Beyond the recheck, it costs you nothing, and you keep every bit of the tooth. In exchange, you need to come back so we can catch any change.

A small filling handles decay that's gone a little deeper. Our job there is to remove only the damaged part and fill it with a tooth-colored material. Everything else stays as it was. For a cavity of modest size, this is the usual answer.

An onlay is a custom piece, made in a lab or in the office, that covers part of the chewing surface. Dentists use onlays on teeth with damage too large for a filling but with healthy walls still standing. Think of it as a partial cover. Compared with a crown, it saves more of your tooth.

Crowns are caps that cover the whole visible tooth. To fit one, the dentist shapes the tooth down on all sides. That's the right call for a tooth that is badly broken, weakened after a root canal, or cracked through much of its structure. Strength is their advantage. They also take the most tooth, and a crowned tooth can't go back to being an uncrowned one.

Given the choice, we'd rather put an onlay on a tooth that qualifies for one than crown it "just to be safe." But if your tooth has a crack that an onlay won't protect, we'll say so and recommend the crown.

### Bonding or a veneer

For chips, small gaps, or worn edges on front teeth, you'll hear about two options.

Bonding is tooth-colored resin that we shape right onto your tooth in one visit. Little or no drilling is needed. Over time it can stain or chip and need a touch-up, but touch-ups are easy, and your tooth underneath stays close to untouched.

Veneers are thin shells, usually porcelain, glued to the front of the tooth. Porcelain resists stains better than resin, and a veneer can change the look of a tooth more than bonding can. Before placing veneers, the dentist removes a thin layer of enamel so they sit flush. There's no undoing that step, so once you have veneers, those teeth will always need some kind of covering.

With a single chipped tooth, we suggest bonding first. Someone who wants to change the shape and color of four or six front teeth may be a good fit for veneers, as long as they understand the change is permanent.

### Root canal, or extraction and an implant

This is the biggest choice patients face, and it's worth slowing down for.

Root canal treatment is for a tooth whose pulp is infected or dying. (Pulp is the soft tissue in the center of the tooth that holds its nerves and blood supply.) During the procedure, the dentist cleans out the inside of the tooth and seals it. Back teeth then get a crown to protect what's left. You keep your own root, set in your own bone, held by your own gum tissue.

The other path is to pull the tooth and replace it. An implant is a small metal post placed in the jawbone that acts as a new root, with a crown attached on top. Implants are a good, proven option. Getting one, though, is surgery, and it takes a series of visits spread over months while the bone heals around the post.

Whenever a tooth can be saved with a reasonable outlook, we'll recommend saving it. Nothing we can place works as well as your natural root. A tooth cracked below the gum line, or one with too little structure left to hold a crown, is a different story, and we'll tell you that rather than put you through a treatment that's likely to fail.

## How cost and time play out over a tooth's life

The short version: the cheapest option today isn't always the cheapest over twenty years, and the reverse is true too.

Small fillings cost less and take less chair time than crowns. If yours holds for years and the tooth stays healthy, you've come out ahead on both money and tooth. Happily, that best case is common.

Every restoration wears out eventually, though. Picture it as a ladder. When a filling fails, the dentist replaces it with a bigger filling. Down the road, a big filling can turn into an onlay or a crown, and a crowned tooth may, much later, need a root canal. Each step up costs more and takes more of the tooth. Starting small keeps you lower on that ladder for longer, and nobody can climb you back down.

Skipping needed treatment runs the math the other way. Ignore a cavity and it can grow until you need a root canal and crown, which costs far more time and money than the filling would have.

So "conservative" and "cheap" aren't the same thing. Careful spending of tooth structure is the point, and over the long run it usually saves money too.

Prices depend on your tooth and your insurance, so we won't guess at them here. Ask at your visit and we'll go through them line by line.

## Questions worth asking any dentist

You should leave every visit understanding what we found and why we're suggesting a treatment. If you don't, ask. Good dentists are glad when patients do.

Here are the ones we'd suggest. What exactly do you see, and can you show me on the X-ray or photo? What happens if I wait six months? Is there a smaller option, and why isn't it the right one here? How long does this kind of repair usually last? And if this were your tooth, what would you do?

Honestly, that last one is our favorite. Few dentists dodge it.

## What a second opinion is (and when to get one)

Getting a second opinion means having another dentist look at the same problem and tell you what they'd recommend. A good dentist won't take offense. It's a normal step, and we think you should get one before any large or permanent treatment, like multiple crowns, veneers, or an extraction.

Bring your recent X-rays if you have them, so you don't need new ones taken. Then compare the two opinions. Where both dentists point to the same problem and the same fix, you can go ahead with confidence. Where they differ, ask each one to walk you through the reasoning.

Second opinions are a regular part of our week. Agreeing with the plan you already have is a common outcome, and we'll say so when it happens.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is conservative dentistry the same as "doing less"?

No. Treatment matches what your tooth needs, and no more. Some patients need a watch-and-wait visit, others a root canal. Either way, the aim is to protect what's healthy.

### Will waiting on a small spot make it worse?

It can, which is why we don't wait and forget. We only watch spots that are still in the enamel, and we recheck them at set visits. If one changes, we treat it while the repair is still small.

### Why did another dentist recommend a crown when you suggested a filling?

Two dentists can weigh the same tooth differently. One may see a crack risk that calls for full coverage, while the other thinks the walls are strong enough for a filling or onlay. Ask both to show you what they see. Any good dentist will welcome the question, and the answers will help you decide.

### Are implants bad?

Not at all. They're a good way to replace a missing tooth. Our preference is to keep a healthy root in place when we can and save the implant for a tooth that can't be saved.

### Does conservative care take more visits?

Sometimes. Watching a spot means a follow-up appointment. On the plus side, you tend to need fewer big procedures overall, and we've yet to meet a patient who'd rather have a crown than a quick check.

### Does "conservative" mean you avoid new technology?

Also no. The word describes how much tooth we remove, not which tools we use. Tooth-colored fillings and bonding are modern options, and they're part of why small repairs work so well today.
