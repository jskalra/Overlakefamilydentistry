Inside every tooth is soft tissue called the pulp, which holds the nerve and blood supply. When deep decay, a crack, or an injury lets bacteria reach it, the pulp can become inflamed or infected. A root canal removes that tissue, cleans the inside of the tooth, and seals it, so the tooth can stay in your mouth.

## Why we try to save the tooth

Pulling the tooth is the other option. It's faster and cheaper on day one. But it leaves a gap that usually needs a bridge, implant, or partial later, and the bone in that spot starts to shrink. A treated tooth keeps your own root in place, and with a crown on top it can last for many years.

Say a back molar has been throbbing for a week and now aches with hot coffee long after the sip. An X-ray shows decay that has reached the nerve. A root canal stops the infection, and most patients notice the pain they walked in with fades within a few days.

## The visit, step by step

We numb the tooth first, and for most people it feels a lot like getting a filling. A small rubber sheet keeps the area dry. We make an opening in the top of the tooth, remove the pulp, then clean and shape each canal. They're filled with a rubber-like material and sealed.

The tooth can be tender for a few days. Back teeth in particular get brittle after treatment, so we usually recommend a crown to protect them from cracking.

Lingering pain, swelling, or sensitivity that doesn't go away are worth getting checked. An exam and X-ray tell us whether you need one.
